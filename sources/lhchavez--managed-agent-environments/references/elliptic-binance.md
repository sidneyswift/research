Oct 30, 2026

Last updated: November 1, 08:00 UTC. This is the final update to this article. We will track the laundering of the stolen funds in a new article.
November 1, 06:00 UTC: Binance announced it has begun reopening withdrawals, starting with $BTC Bitcoin network.
October 31, 23:55 UTC: The FBI has [attributed the Binance theft to North Korea](https://www.fbi.gov/investigate/cyber/alerts/2026/north-korea-responsible-for-6-billion-binance-attack), naming the TraderTraitor group. Elliptic had assessed this as likely. We’ve updated this article to reflect the attribution.

This is a rapidly evolving situation, and we will update this article with our latest findings.
Key points

- Elliptic's analysis shows that $6.43 billion in cryptoassets was stolen from Binance on October 29, 2026, more than four times the $1.46 billion stolen from Bybit in 2025.
- The FBI has attributed the theft to North Korea, naming the TraderTraitor group. Elliptic had assessed TraderTraitor as the likely perpetrator before the announcement.

- Binance initially linked the breach to a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop, but now says it has uncovered additional points of breach in a complex attack that "was not carried out by one person".

- Elliptic has [attributed addresses associated with the theft](https://www.elliptic.co/binance-hack-2026-blocklist/), and customers can screen against them now.
On October 29, 2026, $6.43 billion in cryptoassets were stolen from Binance, the world's largest cryptoasset exchange, according to Elliptic's analysis. Binance has estimated the impact at around $6.3 billion. Over the course of a few hours, the attacker drained Binance hot wallets across 11 blockchains.
This is by far the largest crypto heist of all time. It dwarfs the [$1.46 billion stolen from Bybit](https://www.elliptic.co/insights/bybit-hack-largest-in-history/) in February 2025, which was itself more than twice the size of [any previous crypto theft](https://www.elliptic.co/insights/elliptic-10-year-anniversary-the-biggest-crypto-hacks-of-the-last-decade/). In fact, this incident is almost certainly the single largest known theft of any kind. Before Bybit, that record was held by Saddam Hussein, who took $1 billion from the Central Bank of Iraq in Baghdad on the eve of the 2003 Iraq War. The Binance theft is more than six times larger.

Source: Elliptic. Values at the time of theft. Central Bank of Iraq figure as reported in 2003.

On October 31, the FBI [attributed this theft](https://www.fbi.gov/investigate/cyber/alerts/2026/north-korea-responsible-for-6-billion-binance-attack) to North Korea’s TraderTraitor hacking group, which Elliptic has already assessed as the likely culprit. This single incident almost doubles the total cryptoassets stolen by the regime. Actors linked to North Korea, officially the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea (DPRK), are believed to have [stolen over $6.5 billion](https://www.elliptic.co/insights/drift-protocol-exploited-for-286-million-in-suspected-dprk-linked-attack/) in cryptoassets in recent years, including [more than $1 billion tracked by Elliptic in 2026](https://www.elliptic.co/insights/bitget-attack-pushes-suspected-north-korea-crypto-heists-over-1-billion-in-2026/) before this incident. The US government has linked these thefts to the funding of North Korea’s weapons programs.
At approximately 20:05 UTC on October 29, five Binance wallets on the XRP Ledger sent 233 million XRP to a single attacker-controlled address. Over the next 30 minutes, Binance hot wallets on Solana, NEAR, Base, Optimism, Bitcoin, Tron, Ethereum, Arbitrum and Avalanche were also drained. A second wave of withdrawals hit Binance wallets on Avalanche and Polygon shortly after 23:00 UTC.
The largest losses were on Ethereum, where $3.80 billion was taken across 186 different tokens, including 1.9 billion USDT and 823 million USDC. The attacker also stole 18,270 BTC, worth $1.48 billion, and 612.7 million USDT on Tron. Binance paused withdrawals, which it says stopped the outflows, and is rebuilding its withdrawal service. In a video update on October 30, Binance co-CEO Richard Teng said he was hopeful that withdrawals would resume before Sunday, October 31. Binance has said it will cover all losses using its SAFU fund and company funds, and that it has reserved more than $6.34 billion of company funds to do so. Around $5 billion of this is already held in the same tokens that were stolen, and Binance is buying the rest through the market and OTC trades. It plans to publish a full Proof of Reserves report by Monday.

Source: Elliptic analysis. Stolen amounts from Binance-to-attacker transfers at the time of theft, cross-checked against block explorers and DefiLlama prices. Frozen amounts from on-chain freeze and blacklist events, at current prices.

Four assets made up 74% of the theft: USDT on Ethereum, BTC, USDC and USDT on Tron. Two-thirds of the Ethereum losses, $2.53 billion, left in a single transaction at 20:24 UTC. Elliptic's total excludes a further $169 million that left Binance after withdrawals were suspended. This went to long-established addresses with no link to the attacker, and appears to be Binance moving its own funds.
Binance's initial investigation suggests that the incident "may involve a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop, which allowed an attacker to gain access to a service container and submit unauthorized withdrawal transactions". At that stage, Binance said it had found no evidence of a private key leak. In a later update, Binance said its investigation "has uncovered additional points of breach" and described the incident as "a complex attack". In his video update, Teng added that the attack "was not carried out by one person", and that the attacker used a self-destruct mechanism that destroyed tooling and traces as it ran, making the investigation harder. He said the attacker's use of EIP-7702 delegation, explained below, "did not pass through our normal approval controls". Binance is now carrying out a full security review, but has not yet said what the additional points of breach were.
The on-chain evidence suggests that, rather than stealing Binance's private keys, the attacker was able to get Binance's own systems to sign transactions on their behalf:

- Preparation: Between October 28 and 29, a newly created wallet controlled by the attacker received three withdrawals of 0.1 ETH from the Tornado Cash mixer. At 15:59 UTC October 29, the attacker deployed the "sweeper" contract used in the attack, which was rehearsed on another wallet around four hours before it was used against Binance.
- EIP-7702 delegation: On Ethereum, the attacker abused EIP-7702, a feature introduced in the 2025 Pectra upgrade that allows a regular wallet to delegate its behavior to a smart contract. At 20:20 UTC, the attacker submitted a single transaction carrying 50 authorizations, all validly signed with the key of the Binance hot wallet known as Binance 14. Each covered a different nonce, ensuring that one would match the wallet's transaction count when it landed. This delegated control of Binance 14 to the attacker's sweeper contract, which three and a half minutes later emptied it of 167,464 ETH and 63 tokens, including 1.6 billion USDT.
- Persistent access: The delegation remained in place, so no new signatures were needed. The attacker used Binance 14's owner rights over another Binance wallet to pull 823 million USDC from it, and swept new deposits into Binance 14 four more times, the last at 23:56 UTC.
- Other blockchains: On the ten other affected blockchains, funds were sent from Binance wallets in ordinary signed transactions. On Ethereum, another hot wallet, Binance 16, also sent 28,006 ETH and other tokens worth $174 million in total directly to the attacker, signed with its own key. This suggests the attacker was able to instruct Binance's signing infrastructure directly.
This closely resembles the Bybit theft, in which North Korean hackers compromised a developer's machine at the wallet provider Safe{Wallet} and manipulated the transaction that Bybit's signers approved. In both cases, it was the infrastructure and people around the wallets that were compromised, not the cryptography.

The first step in laundering stolen cryptoassets is typically to exchange any tokens for a "native" blockchain asset such as Ether or Bitcoin. This is because tokens have issuers who can in some cases freeze wallets containing stolen assets, whereas there is no central party who can freeze Ether or Bitcoin.
Stablecoins made up $3.46 billion of the stolen funds, and the attacker was slow to convert them. The stolen USDT reached the attacker's main Ethereum wallet at 20:24 UTC. Tether froze that wallet 37 minutes later, locking 1.18 billion USDT in place, and then followed the funds, freezing four more attacker addresses within 17 minutes. Circle froze the 823 million USDC in the same wallet three minutes after that. Ripple, World Liberty Financial, Paxos and First Digital froze further stablecoins, Tether froze tokenized gold, Binance froze the WBETH it issues, and the issuers of several smaller tokens froze their own tokens. The Arbitrum Security Council took emergency action to move assets out of the attacker's reach, and on October 31 Optimism's governance appears to have done the same, moving 44 million OP (around $5 million) out of the attacker's address. In total, Elliptic has verified $2.85 billion of frozen or seized funds on-chain, close to Binance's estimate of around $3 billion.

Source: Elliptic analysis of on-chain freezes, seizures and attacker balances, about 05:50 UTC on November 1, 2026, at current prices. Excludes an Aave position holding $59.9M of collateral.

Stablecoins make up 94% of the frozen funds, almost entirely on Ethereum and Tron. Arbitrum and Optimism are the only blockchains where everything stolen has been secured, in both cases by the network's own governance. Nothing stolen on Bitcoin, the XRP Ledger, Solana or NEAR has been frozen, because native assets have no issuer that can freeze them. Ripple, for example, can freeze RLUSD but not XRP. Aave froze its markets, but this blocked new activity rather than seizing any of the attacker's funds.
Before the freezes landed, the attacker converted around $786 million of stablecoins into ETH and TRX. They have also been working to convert and move the remaining funds:

- Stablecoin swaps: On Ethereum, 434 million USDT was swapped for 160,451 ETH through Uniswap, 1inch and market makers' request-for-quote systems. The swaps implied a loss of 12% to 26% against the market price, suggesting the attacker was prioritizing speed over value.
- DeFi lending: A second attacker wallet deposited 59.9 million USDT into Aave as collateral and borrowed 19,762 ETH, before Aave froze the affected markets. The loan is still open and close to the point of liquidation.
- DEX trading: On Tron, 234 million USDT was swapped for 638 million TRX on SunSwap. On Ethereum, 48 million RLUSD was sold before Ripple froze the remainder, and around 150 other tokens were dumped for ETH on decentralized exchanges. These token sales stopped at around 06:36 UTC on October 30.
- Bitcoin: The 18,270 BTC has been split through a short peel chain into 19 addresses, most holding 1,000 BTC each. None of it has yet been sent to a service.
- Cross-chain activity: On October 30, the attacker began moving funds between blockchains. Between 11:38 and 12:29 UTC, they sold all of the stolen AVAX for USDC, bridged it to Ethereum using Circle's Cross-Chain Transfer Protocol (CCTP), and swapped it for 7,242.8 ETH.
- Tron: On the morning of October 30, the 638 million TRX was split across five new addresses. Over the following two days, all of it was passed to a single wallet, which peeled it off in small amounts to dozens of fresh wallets and was almost empty by the morning of November 1. From there, the TRX has been swapped through decentralized exchanges and a lending protocol, and sent across at least six cross-chain bridges, almost all of it ending up as ETH on Ethereum. Elliptic has matched around $122 million of bridge outputs so far. Tether has blacklisted some of the receiving addresses, so that they cannot hold USDT. Elliptic has confirmed that some of the Tron funds have been laundered alongside proceeds of the September 2026 exploit of Bitget, as first reported by ZachXBT.
At current prices, the attacker now controls around $3.25 billion of assets that cannot be frozen by an issuer, spread across more than 800 addresses. This includes around 584,000 ETH ($1.32 billion), 18,327 BTC ($1.42 billion) and 233 million XRP. The ETH includes TRX laundered through bridges, which arrives on Ethereum and is split across hundreds of fresh wallets. Almost all of the stolen TRX has now been laundered. The XRP, SOL and NEAR, worth around $439 million, have not moved at all since they were stolen. Based on the [laundering of the Bybit funds](https://www.elliptic.co/insights/bybit-exploit-six-months-on/), we expect these assets to be layered through many wallets, cross-chain bridges, mixers and swap services, before attempts are made to cash them out through OTC brokers and exchanges.

The pattern of transactions suggests that much of the laundering was carried out by software rather than by hand. Between October 30 and November 1, the attacker's main Tron laundering wallet sent out TRX in 1,113 separate transfers, until it was almost empty. The median gap between transfers was 90 seconds, and until the wallet began to run dry, the longest pause was 39 minutes. The activity continued through both nights in Pyongyang.

Source: Elliptic analysis of TronGrid data for the attacker's main Tron laundering wallet, October 30 02:00 UTC to November 1 05:33 UTC.

Other signs point the same way:

- Randomized amounts: Almost every transfer is for an irregular amount, down to six decimal places. This is typical of laundering scripts built to evade simple monitoring rules.
- Fast hand-offs: The wallets receiving these funds typically pass them on within about two minutes, and some within 30 seconds.
- Time-sliced selling: On Ethereum, stolen tokens were sold in dozens of small pieces. LINK was sold in 89 pieces and PEPE in 94, around 36 seconds apart.
- Templated set-up: New wallets used to sell tokens were each funded with exactly 0.5 ETH for fees, and began trading within minutes.
Some steps still look like human decisions. These include small test transactions before large moves, round-number transfers between the attacker's own wallets and trials of different exit services. Whether the automation involves AI agents, rather than conventional scripts, cannot be determined from the on-chain data. An agent might be revealed by routes that change quickly in response to freezes and blocklist updates, or by trial-and-error patterns of failed transactions. We have seen bursts of failed bridge transactions from the same wallets, but these look more like a script retrying without fixing the cause than an agent learning from its mistakes.

On October 31, the FBI attributed the theft to TraderTraitor, its name for a North Korean state-sponsored hacking group. This confirms Elliptic's earlier assessment. The FBI has previously attributed the Bybit and DMM Bitcoin thefts to TraderTraitor. Together with the US Secret Service and IRS Criminal Investigation, it has observed TraderTraitor converting the stolen assets primarily into ETH, and expects them to be laundered further through DeFi and instant swap services. The FBI has published the addresses involved, and urged exchanges, DeFi services, blockchain analytics firms and others to block transactions linked to them.
Elliptic's assessment was based on the following factors:

- Links to the Bitget exploit: Elliptic has confirmed that funds stolen from Binance are being laundered alongside proceeds of the [Bitget exploit](https://www.elliptic.co/insights/bitget-attack-pushes-suspected-north-korea-crypto-heists-over-1-billion-in-2026/), which we assessed as highly likely to be DPRK-linked. The two sets of funds have now been combined in several wallets, and the amount has grown overnight. The link was first reported by ZachXBT.
- Links to earlier TraderTraitor thefts: Elliptic has identified common service usage between those laundering the Binance funds and those behind three earlier thefts attributed to TraderTraitor, from BigONE, WOO X and AFX.
- Methods: The long preparation, the apparent targeting of staff and the use of Binance's own systems to sign transactions resemble earlier TraderTraitor thefts, including Bybit.
- Scale: Very few actors could coordinate an attack across 11 blockchains in around three hours. Most of the largest exchange and bridge hacks of recent years have been attributed to DPRK-linked actors.
The FBI's attribution confirms Elliptic's assessment. We will continue to check for links to other DPRK-attributed thefts as the stolen funds move, and will report on this in our new article tracking the laundering.
